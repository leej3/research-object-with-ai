# Core argument

## Central question

> What properties do we want the things we make with AI to have?

Knowledge workers create digital artifacts: reports, assessments,
presentations, analyses, guides, legal documents, software, scientific results,
and many other things. AI makes it possible to produce many more of these
artifacts, much more quickly.

The visible output is only part of an artifact's value. We also need to
consider whether its quality can be assessed, whether its history can be
understood, and whether its useful parts can participate appropriately in
future work.

## The argument

### Here are things we make with AI

AI may help create a consultant's report, a clinical assessment, a blog post,
a flier, a presentation, a style guide, or a scientific analysis. These are not
merely pieces of content. They are objects that people may need to examine,
apply, preserve, adapt, combine, or learn from.

Different artifacts have different futures. A style guide is intended for
repeated use. A presentation may be adapted for a new audience. A house survey
or clinical assessment may need to remain a fixed record, while its evidence,
method, terminology, or structure remains relevant to later work. Reuse does
not always mean editing the original object.

### AI may get us 80% of the way

“Eighty percent” is an intuition rather than a measurement. AI can quickly
produce something that looks substantially complete, while an expert supplies
the remaining judgment, correction, selection, and responsibility.

The difficult question is not simply what the final 20% contains. It is how we
assess the quality of the whole. A polished output does not reveal which parts
are sound, which assumptions were made, what the expert checked, what changed,
or where important weaknesses remain.

This problem is not exclusive to AI. Human work can also conceal its sources,
history, and construction. AI makes the limitation harder to ignore because it
increases the speed, volume, and apparent completeness of what can be
produced.

### Reputation has traditionally carried part of the burden

We often accept an opinion or artifact partly because a recognized expert or
institution stands behind it. Reputation gives us a reason to take seriously
work whose construction we cannot inspect completely.

Reputation remains valuable, but it cannot carry the entire burden when the
volume of content grows and authorship becomes more complicated. The artifact
itself can provide additional grounds for assessment by showing enough of its
work.

That may include:

- sources and inputs;
- assumptions and instructions;
- versions and changes;
- methods and calculations;
- relationships among components;
- checks that were performed;
- the environment in which the work was produced;
- enough information to repeat or test relevant procedures.

Exposing these things does not prove that the work is good. A reproducible
result can still be wrong. It makes the quality of the work more open to
examination. The willingness to permit that examination can itself be a useful
signal.

### Here are properties that make artifacts more useful

An artifact becomes more useful when it carries enough information and
structure for people to understand what it is, assess its quality, and use it
appropriately.

Depending on the object, we may want to ask:

- Is everything necessary available?
- Can we see where it came from and how it changed?
- Can a person or machine act on it or repeat a relevant procedure?
- Can its components be understood or changed independently?
- Can it work in another environment?
- Can we distinguish its requirements from hidden local leftovers?
- Can someone else obtain the same usable object?

Not every artifact should maximize every property. A clinical record may need
to remain immutable and private. Another object may be intentionally temporary.
The point is to make deliberate choices about what the artifact needs to be.

### More artifacts can become a system of knowledge

The opportunity created by AI is not merely to produce more deliverables. An
organization can develop a growing body of work that people and AI agents can
draw upon.

For that body of work to function as a useful system, its artifacts must carry
enough evidence and structure to support:

- human assessment of quality;
- focused expert review;
- discovery and appropriate reuse of components;
- routine automated checks;
- understanding of how current work developed;
- future work by people, agents, or both.

Machines may not be able to determine whether a conclusion is true. They can,
however, check whether required materials are present, trace changes, rerun
procedures, compare outputs, identify missing dependencies, and present
evidence for expert review. Better-structured artifacts make more of this
possible.

## Why open science matters

Open science provides the value system and practical setting for this
argument. Science advances through scrutiny and cumulative work. Conclusions
become more useful when others can examine the evidence, methods, data,
software, and decisions behind them.

Different industries have different reasons to restrict access, preserve
ownership, or keep parts of their work private. Science has especially strong
reasons to pursue openness, while still respecting ethical, legal, privacy, and
security constraints: as open as possible, as closed as necessary.

Science also makes these properties unusually difficult to achieve. A
scientific result may depend on expensive data collection, specialized
judgment, multiple datasets, software, computational environments, parameters,
procedures, and people distributed across different places. The paper or chart
is only the visible surface of the work.

This makes science a demanding and revealing domain in which to ask how
knowledge artifacts should be organized.

## The role of STAMPED

STAMPED is not the argument itself. It is the vocabulary our group uses to
describe properties the audience has already encountered through ordinary
examples.

The talk should therefore teach the concepts before revealing their names. The
audience first develops an intuition for wanting an object to be complete,
traceable, actionable, separable into useful parts, movable, independent of
hidden state, and shareable. STAMPED then gives explicit language to those
ideas:

- **Self-contained**
- **Tracked**
- **Actionable**
- **Modular**
- **Portable**
- **Ephemeral**
- **Distributable**

The reveal should feel like recognition rather than the introduction of seven
definitions to memorize.

## Narrative progression

The talk can be summarized as:

> Here are things we make with AI.
>
> The finished output does not tell us enough about the quality of the work.
>
> Here are properties that make artifacts more useful and open to assessment.
>
> AI increases the number of artifacts and the need to reason about those
> properties.
>
> Open science has especially strong reasons to pursue them.
>
> Science also makes them unusually difficult to achieve.
>
> STAMPED gives us language for designing and evaluating the result.

The audience should leave with a broader understanding of “useful.” It is not
only whether an artifact answers today's question or looks finished. It is
also whether the object provides enough evidence and structure for its quality
to be assessed and for its useful knowledge to contribute appropriately to
future work.

## Concise formulation

> AI can help us produce something that looks finished. A well-constructed
> artifact shows enough of its work for someone to assess its quality and
> understand how it can be used.

STAMPED is our approach to thinking systematically about what such an artifact
should provide in science. The same questions apply to many other kinds of
knowledge work.
