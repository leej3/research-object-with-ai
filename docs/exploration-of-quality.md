A Lessig-style sequence is a good fit here: one claim at a time, sparse, cumulative, with occasional reversals that sharpen the distinctions.

Here’s a first stream:

We produce artifacts.

Documents.
Code.
Analyses.
Decisions.

Artifacts have quality.

They can be correct.
Complete.
Useful.
Fit for purpose.

But quality is not directly observable.

We assess it.

Assessment costs effort.

Reading.
Checking.
Recalculating.
Following citations.
Testing assumptions.

So we use shortcuts.

Reputation.
Credentials.
Experience.
Presentation.
Institution.

These shortcuts produce confidence.

But confidence is not quality.

Confidence can be wrong.

Experts can be overconfident.

Experts can be underconfident.

What we want is calibrated confidence.

High confidence when quality is high.

Low confidence when quality is low.

Evidence helps.

Sources.
Calculations.
Tests.
Reviews.
Intermediate work.

Evidence provides assurance.

Assurance is not confidence.

It is the grounds for justified confidence.

But evidence can exist without being usable.

A thousand citations.

A million log entries.

A complete audit trail nobody can understand.

So assurance is not enough.

We also need assessability.

Assessability is about effort.

How readily can someone determine the quality of the work?

High assurance can have low assessability.

The evidence exists.

Finding the answer takes three weeks.

High assessability can have low assurance.

A beautiful dashboard.

A neat provenance graph.

Weak evidence underneath.

The goal is both.

Strong evidence.

Cheap to interrogate.

This matters for human work.

Much knowledge work has surprisingly little provenance.

We receive the final document.

We infer the process.

We trust the author.

The author is part of the assurance system.

“She knows what she’s doing.”

“I trust his analysis.”

“Legal reviewed it.”

AI changes this.

The producer becomes less informative.

“This came from GPT-5” tells us relatively little about this particular output.

And AI is persuasive.

Fluency looks like competence.

Specificity looks like knowledge.

Coherence looks like correctness.

AI can therefore increase confidence without increasing assurance.

That is dangerous.

But AI also creates an opportunity.

AI work is computational work.

Computational work can leave traces.

The source was retrieved.

The calculation was executed.

The file was changed.

The test failed.

The artifact was revised.

The test passed.

These are not explanations of what the AI was thinking.

They are records of what happened.

That distinction matters.

Generated rationale is another output.

Provenance is evidence of the production process.

Preserve the process.

Not necessarily the hidden reasoning.

The observable work.

Now the final artifact is no longer alone.

It arrives with evidence.

And that evidence can increase assurance.

We have better grounds for judging quality.

Structure that evidence well, and assessability increases too.

The reviewer does less reconstruction.

This changes the economics of review.

Today:

Producing work is expensive.

Checking work is expensive.

AI makes producing work cheap.

But checking it may remain expensive.

That creates a bottleneck.

The scarce resource becomes human evaluation.

So faster generation is not enough.

We need cheaper verification.

Provenance can make verification cheaper.

Not by eliminating judgment.

By directing it.

The expert no longer asks:

“Can I reproduce this entire analysis?”

They can ask:

“Show me why this claim is here.”

“Show me the source.”

“Show me the calculation.”

“Show me what changed.”

“Show me what was tested.”

Expertise becomes leverage rather than labor.

The human investigates where judgment matters.

The system exposes evidence where reconstruction would otherwise be necessary.

This should improve calibration.

Not:

Trust the AI.

But:

Make it cheap to know when to trust the work.

And perhaps that is the deeper opportunity.

AI can make artifacts cheaper to produce.

But AI-native workflows could make artifacts cheaper to assess.

The important productivity gain may therefore not just be generation.

It may be assessability.

And if assessability lets humans make better use of strong evidence—

better calibration.

Which means we can scale not merely the production of knowledge work,

but our ability to trust it appropriately.
